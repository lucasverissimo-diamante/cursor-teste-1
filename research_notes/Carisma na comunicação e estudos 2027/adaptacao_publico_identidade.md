# Adapting speech to the interlocutor/audience and connecting through shared identity: what the research says about charisma

Scope note for the report writer: the two cases are (a) a speaker from Minas Gerais who, after a year of contact with people from Pará, spontaneously wrote "chegastes a me ligar ontem?" to a person from Pará after imagining a conversation with them; (b) the same speaker, who practises Candomblé (orixá Exu), saying "Deus" instead of "Exu" in a lecture at a family company that opened with the Lord's Prayer, and reporting intense audience attention.

## 1. Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT): convergence, divergence, over-accommodation, and how listeners judge accommodation

### Takeaway
CAT holds that speakers converge toward (or diverge from) interlocutors to manage social distance, approval and group identity. Listeners usually reward convergence, but not automatically: the evaluation depends on the **motive they infer** (effort and goodwill vs. external pressure, condescension or manipulation) and on **dosage**. Convergence that is "too much", or based on stereotypes of the other, is read as over-accommodation (patronizing, mocking or fake).

### Cited Findings
- **Origins.** Giles, Taylor & Bourhis (1973), "Towards a theory of interpersonal accommodation through language: Some Canadian data," *Language in Society* 2(2), 177–192. In bilingual Montreal, the more a speaker accommodated linguistically (English→French), the more favourably listeners rated them and the more they reciprocated — [doi:10.1017/S0047404500000701](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0047404500000701) [PK].
- **Attribution of intent decides the evaluation.** Simard, Taylor & Giles (1976), "Attribution processes and interpersonal accommodation in a bilingual setting," *Language and Speech* 19(4), 374–387. Convergence was rated more positively when listeners attributed it to the speaker's wish to bridge cultural differences than when they attributed it to external pressure. Non-convergence was judged less harshly when it was attributed to situational constraints — [doi:10.1177/002383097601900408](https://doi.org/10.1177/002383097601900408) [PK].
- **"Optimal" rather than maximal convergence.** Giles & Smith (1979), "Accommodation theory: Optimal levels of convergence," in H. Giles & R. St Clair (eds.), *Language and Social Psychology* (Oxford: Blackwell), pp. 45–65. Convergence on all dimensions at once (pronunciation + speech rate + message content) was rated less favourably than partial convergence. This is the classic early evidence that over-convergence backfires [PK; no URL verified].
- **Canonical statement of the theory.** Giles, H., Coupland, N., & Coupland, J. (1991), "Accommodation theory: Communication, context, and consequence," in Giles, Coupland & Coupland (eds.), *Contexts of Accommodation: Developments in Applied Sociolinguistics* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press / Paris: Éditions de la Maison des Sciences de l'Homme), pp. 1–68 [PK; no URL verified]. Key ideas: convergence, divergence and maintenance; accommodation as a strategy for seeking approval or efficiency, or for signalling group identity; over- and under-accommodation as "miscommunication."
- **Intercultural formulation.** Gallois, C., Ogay, T., & Giles, H. (2005), "Communication accommodation theory: A look back and a look ahead," in W. B. Gudykunst (ed.), *Theorizing About Intercultural Communication* (Thousand Oaks, CA: Sage), pp. 121–148 [PK]. Adds the speaker's "initial orientation" (interpersonal vs. intergroup), the sociohistorical context, and the listener's evaluation and attribution as formal stages of the model.
- **Strategies beyond accent.** Dragojevic, Gasiorek & Giles (2016), "Accommodative strategies as core of the theory," a chapter in H. Giles (ed.), *Communication Accommodation Theory: Negotiating Personal Relationships and Social Identities across Contexts* (Cambridge University Press) — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/communication-accommodation-theory/accommodative-strategies-as-core-of-the-theory/32D969C90EA99611BAF1427994461EC7) [V, existence]. Content [PK]: accommodation covers approximation (accent, dialect, rate), interpretability, discourse management, interpersonal control and emotional expression. Choosing a shared religious word is therefore a form of accommodation in CAT terms, not only a lexical choice.
- The same volume has a chapter on the "dark side" of CAT: non-, over- and under-accommodation — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/communication-accommodation-theory/dark-side-of-cat/0B6D0B930E95BC9C2C4ED19C9A7CC6DB) [V, existence; authorship believed to be Gasiorek, PK].
- **Inferred motive (modern evidence).** Gasiorek, J., & Giles, H. (2012), "Effects of inferred motive on evaluations of nonaccommodative communication," *Human Communication Research* 38, 309–332 — [doi:10.1111/j.1468-2958.2012.01426.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2012.01426.x) [V, bibliographic]. Content [PK]: listeners' judgments of non-accommodation depend on whether they infer a positive or negative motive. A follow-up on motive inference appeared in *Western Journal of Communication* 79 (2015), 456–471 — [reference listing](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/08934215.2017.1335760?scroll=top) [V, bibliographic].
- **Meta-analysis.** Soliz & Giles (2014) meta-analysed CAT research up to 2010. According to a 2015 editorial by Gasiorek, Giles & Soliz in *Language & Communication*, "more than half of all CAT research has been completed since 2000" — [core.ac.uk PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/84309575.pdf) [V]. The full reference is believed to be Soliz, J., & Giles, H. (2014), "Relational and identity processes in communication: A contextual and meta-analytical review of communication accommodation theory," *Communication Yearbook* 38, 107–144 [PK — title, volume and pages not confirmed; the search could not surface the article]. The Cambridge CAT book has an "Effect Size Meta-Analysis" section and a "Meta-Analytical Data" appendix drawn from it — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/9781316226537%23CT-BP-10/type/BOOK_PART) [V].
- **Latest review.** Giles, H., Edwards, A. L., & Walther, J. B. (2023), "Communication accommodation theory: Past accomplishments, current trends, and future prospects," *Language Sciences* 99, 101571. Part of the journal's 50th-anniversary issue. It proposes a refined and expanded set of "Principles of Accommodation" and a seventh stage of CAT focused on computer-mediated and human–machine communication — [doi:10.1016/j.langsci.2023.101571](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.langsci.2023.101571); [UKY record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/communication-accommodation-theory-past-accomplishments-current-t/) [V].
- **Accent imitation can raise liking, but not for every accent.** Adank, P., Stewart, A. J., Connell, L., & Wood, J. (2013), *Frontiers in Psychology* 4:280. Imitating a speaker's accent raised that speaker's perceived social attractiveness compared with repeating in one's own accent. A follow-up found the effect for one accent but not another — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00280](https://dx.doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00280); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3659325); [ICPhS 2015 paper](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1468561/1/ICPhS15_fullpaper.pdf) [V]. (Paper title believed to be "Accent imitation positively affects language attitudes" [PK].)
- **Patronizing over-accommodation** (e.g., "elderspeak") is the classic negative case. Ryan, Giles, Bartolucci & Henwood (1986), "Psycholinguistic and social psychological components of communication by and with the elderly," *Language & Communication* 6(1–2), 1–24 [PK].

### Inferences
- Evaluation sits on two axes. The first is **perceived motive**: affiliation and effort read as warm; ingratiation, pressure or stereotype read as fake or patronizing. The second is **calibration**: partial, natural-sounding convergence works better than wholesale imitation. A single dialect form produced spontaneously in a private message to a friend ("chegastes") is low-dose, private and plausibly read as affection. Theatrical, accent-wide imitation in front of an audience carries more mockery risk.
- CAT treats religious vocabulary choices as accommodation (discourse management or approximation of the audience's "interpretive frame"). The same evaluation logic applies: the audience's inference about *why* the speaker said "Deus" would determine whether it reads as respect or as concealment (see §10).

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found in which listeners rate dialect or accent imitation as *mocking* versus *affiliative*. Online commentary claims exaggerated convergence is perceived as mocking, but that is not experimental evidence ([Britannica story](https://www.britannica.com/story/what-makes-us-subconsciously-mimic-the-accents-of-others-inconversation); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/neuropsych/subconsciously-mimic-accents/)).
- Effect sizes from the Soliz & Giles meta-analysis could not be retrieved.
- No study was found testing listener evaluations of a speaker who adopts *another region's Brazilian Portuguese morphology* (e.g., a mineiro using paraense "tu" forms).

## 2. Audience design (Allan Bell) and common ground (Herbert Clark)

### Takeaway
Bell's audience design says style shifts are mainly responses to *who the audience is*, including absent "referees" whose speech the speaker orients to. Clark's work says speakers tailor words to the **common ground** they share with addressees, including "communal" common ground based on shared community membership (region, religion, profession). Both frameworks predict the two episodes: a dialect form chosen for a paraense addressee, and a shared-faith word ("Deus") chosen for a Christian audience.

### Cited Findings
- Bell, A. (1984), "Language style as audience design," *Language in Society* 13(2), 145–204 — [doi:10.1017/S004740450001037X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S004740450001037X) [PK]. Core claims [PK]:
  - Intra-speaker style variation derives from, and mirrors, inter-speaker social variation (the "style axiom").
  - Speakers design style mainly for the audience, ranked by role: addressee > auditor > overhearer > eavesdropper.
  - Evidence: the same New Zealand radio newsreaders shifted phonological variables (e.g., intervocalic /t/ voicing) depending on which station, and so which audience, they were reading for.
  - **Referee design**: speakers sometimes shift toward an absent reference group they identify with or want to evoke ("initiative" style).
- Bell, A. (2001), "Back in style: Reworking audience design," in P. Eckert & J. R. Rickford (eds.), *Style and Sociolinguistic Variation* (Cambridge UP), pp. 139–169 [PK].
- Clark, H. H., & Murphy, G. L. (1982), "Audience design in meaning and reference," in J.-F. Le Ny & W. Kintsch (eds.), *Language and Comprehension* (Amsterdam: North-Holland), pp. 287–299 [PK]. Speakers design utterances so that the particular addressee can understand them, given what they mutually know.
- Clark, H. H. (1996), *Using Language* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press) — [doi:10.1017/CBO9780511620539](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511620539) [PK]. Clark distinguishes:
  - **communal common ground**: assumed from shared cultural communities such as nationality, region, language, religion or occupation;
  - **personal common ground**: built from joint experience with a person.

  Language use is a joint activity in which participants coordinate on common ground. Related: Clark & Marshall (1981), "Definite reference and mutual knowledge," in Joshi, Webber & Sag (eds.), *Elements of Discourse Understanding* (Cambridge UP), which introduced "community co-membership" as a basis for mutual knowledge [PK]. Also Clark & Brennan (1991), "Grounding in communication," in Resnick, Levine & Teasley (eds.), *Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition* (APA), pp. 127–149 [PK].
- **Limits of audience design.** Horton, W. S., & Keysar, B. (1996), "When do speakers take into account common ground?" *Cognition* 59, 91–117 — [doi:10.1016/0010-0277(96)81418-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(96)81418-1) [PK]. Under time pressure, speakers' initial plans were less tailored to the addressee's perspective. Audience design is partly effortful, a "monitoring and adjustment" process rather than a default.

### Inferences
- The "Deus" choice is a textbook appeal to **communal common ground**. The ritual opening (the Lord's Prayer) displayed the audience's religious community publicly, and the speaker selected the referring expression in that shared repertoire. In Bell's terms the company's employees are the addressees, and the Christian frame set by the opening prayer works like a "referee" that the speaker styles toward.
- The "chegastes" case looks like audience design toward a single addressee whose regional speech had become part of the speaker's **personal common ground** after a year of contact. Bell's referee design also covers styling toward a group (paraenses) the speaker has come to identify with.
- Horton & Keysar suggest deliberate audience design is effortful. The spontaneous, unplanned character of "chegastes" points instead to automatic mechanisms (§3–4) working together with audience design.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the text of Bell (1984) in this session. The page ranges and the newsreader details are from prior knowledge.
- No study was found that specifically tests audience design of religious vocabulary in workplace talks.

## 3. Automatic alignment, mimicry and language style matching (LSM), and replication status

### Takeaway
Interlocutors align automatically at many linguistic levels (Pickering & Garrod), and people unconsciously mimic one another (Chartrand & Bargh). Function-word style matching (LSM) tracks cohesion and attraction. However, the link from mimicry to liking is **weaker and more conditional** than the 1999 paper suggested. Replications are mixed, and LSM–relationship effects in pooled data are small (r ≈ .10).

### Cited Findings
- **Interactive alignment model.** Pickering, M. J., & Garrod, S. (2004), "Toward a mechanistic psychology of dialogue," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 27(2), 169–190 — [doi:10.1017/S0140525X04000056](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X04000056) [PK]. Claims:
  - Dialogue succeeds because interlocutors' representations become aligned at multiple levels (phonological, lexical, syntactic, situation model).
  - Alignment happens largely through automatic priming.
  - Alignment at one level boosts alignment at others.
  - Repeated expressions become "routinized."
- Related experiment: Branigan, Pickering & Cleland (2000), "Syntactic co-ordination in dialogue," *Cognition* 75(2), B13–B25 [PK].
- **Prediction by simulation.** Pickering, M. J., & Garrod, S. (2013), "An integrated theory of language production and comprehension," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 36(4), 329–347 — [doi:10.1017/S0140525X12001495](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12001495) [PK]. Comprehenders predict others' utterances by covertly simulating them with their own production system. This gives a mechanistic route by which *imagining* an interlocutor activates that interlocutor's forms.
- **Chameleon effect.** Chartrand, T. L., & Bargh, J. A. (1999), "The chameleon effect: The perception–behavior link and social interaction," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 76(6), 893–910 — [PubMed 10402679](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10402679/); [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.76.6.893](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.76.6.893/) [V]. Three experiments [PK for experiment details]:
  1. Participants nonconsciously mimicked a confederate's face-rubbing or foot-shaking.
  2. Being mimicked increased liking and perceived smoothness of the interaction.
  3. Dispositional perspective-takers mimicked more.
- **Replication status: mixed.** The FORRT replication atlas lists Förster et al. (2022), "The Chameleon Effect: A Replication Study," with a **mixed** outcome: mimicry was found for smiling but not for foot-shaking or face-rubbing — [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.76.6.893/) [V]. No Many Labs test of the chameleon effect was found.
- **Responsiveness, not copying per se?** A 2023 *Polish Psychological Bulletin* study added a "responsiveness" control condition. It argues that the contrast between unresponsiveness (the standard control) and simple responsiveness, rather than mimicry itself, may explain mimicry's effect on liking — [PPB 2023 PDF](https://journals.pan.pl//Content/127821/PDF/2023-01-PPB-05.pdf) [V; authors not captured].
- **Affiliation goals increase mimicry.** Lakin, J. L., & Chartrand, T. L. (2003), "Using nonconscious behavioral mimicry to create affiliation and rapport," *Psychological Science* 14(4), 334–339 — [doi:10.1111/1467-9280.14481](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.14481) [PK]. Participants with an (even nonconscious) goal to affiliate mimicked more. A 2023 *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior* article summarizes the bidirectional loop: "pre-existing affiliations between interaction partners elicit mimicry, and in turn, mimicry elicits affiliation" — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10919-023-00446-5) [V, secondary].
- **Strategic mimicry plus perspective-taking.** Maddux, W. W., Mullen, E., & Galinsky, A. D. (2008), "Chameleons bake bigger pies and take bigger pieces: Strategic behavioral mimicry facilitates negotiation outcomes," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 44, 461–468 — [SSOAR PDF](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/bitstream/document/21568/1/ssoar-jexpsocpsychol-2008-2-maddux_et_al-chameleons_bake_bigger_pies_and.pdf); [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2008/chameleons-bake-bigger-pies-and-take-bigger-pieces-strategic) [V]. Negotiators told to mimic subtly got better individual and joint outcomes, and trust mediated the mimicry→deal effect. (Mimicry here was deliberate, so it works even when strategic, provided it is *subtle*.)
- **Language style matching.**
  - Ireland, M. E., & Pennebaker, J. W. (2010), "Language style matching in writing: Synchrony in essays, correspondence, and poetry," *JPSP* 99(3), 549–571 — [doi:10.1037/a0020386](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020386) [PK]. LSM is computed from function-word categories, and matching tracked relationship closeness in historical correspondence and in poet couples.
  - Ireland, M. E., Slatcher, R. B., Eastwick, P. W., Scissors, L. E., Finkel, E. J., & Pennebaker, J. W. (2011), "Language style matching predicts relationship initiation and stability," *Psychological Science* 22(1), 39–44 — [PubMed 21149854](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21149854/) [V, existence]. Content [PK]: in speed dates, higher LSM predicted mutual desire for later contact; in couples' instant messages, higher LSM predicted still being together ~3 months later.
  - Gonzales, A. L., Hancock, J. T., & Pennebaker, J. W. (2010), "Language style matching as a predictor of social dynamics in small groups," *Communication Research* 37(1), 3–19 — [doi:10.1177/0093650209351468](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650209351468) [PK]. LSM predicted group cohesiveness and, in task groups, performance.
- **LSM effects are small when pooled.** An integrative data analysis (Bierstetel et al., *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, 2020) across 766 individuals found the LSM–satisfaction association was only marginal (mean r = .10) — [JSPR 2020 PDF](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/bierstetel_et_al_jspr_2020.pdf) [V].
- **Matching is not always positive.** Negotiation work interprets LSM as heightened engagement or attention, which can be positive or negative depending on context — [search summary of negotiation LSM study](https://ncmr.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/215/galley/218/view) [V, secondary].
- **Phonetic convergence.** Pardo, J. S. (2006), "On phonetic convergence during conversational interaction," *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 119(4), 2382–2393 — [doi:10.1121/1.2178720](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2178720) [PK]. Talkers converge phonetically in conversation, but asymmetrically: it is modulated by role and gender.

### Inferences
- Alignment is the best mechanistic account of *how* "chegastes" could surface without deliberation. A year of exposure stored paraense forms, imagining the interlocutor activated them (simulation and priming), and the output aligned to the imagined partner.
- The evidence base is uneven for the charisma claim that mimicry produces liking. Robust: people align and mimic. Real but small or conditional: being matched raises liking, cohesion and trust. The report should not present "mirroring" as a reliable persuasion technique.
- Attention may be the common factor. Both LSM and rapport research link matching to attentional engagement, which fits the speaker's report of "intensely attentive" audiences without proving causation.

### Gaps
- No recent (2023–2026) meta-analysis of mimicry→liking or of LSM was found in this session.
- The venue of Förster et al. (2022) was not confirmed. The FORRT atlas entry is the only source used.
- The authors of the 2023 PPB study were not captured.

## 4. Second-dialect acquisition (SDA) in adults, and perspective-taking or mental simulation of the interlocutor as a driver of accommodation

### Takeaway
Adults do pick up features of a second dialect after sustained contact, though usually partially. Lexical and morphological items are acquired more easily than complex phonology. Perspective-taking, affiliation motives and *imagined* interactions all raise the likelihood that a speaker produces the interlocutor's forms. Imagining a conversation with a specific person is a plausible trigger for activating that person's dialect.

### Cited Findings
- **Siegel (2010).** Siegel, J. (2010), *Second Dialect Acquisition*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. Reviewed as "the first book devoted exclusively to the field of second dialect acquisition (SDA)." The reviewer notes that subjects in naturalistic settings mostly did not reach native-like acquisition, though some did — [LINGUIST List 22.2926](https://linguistlist.org/issues/22/2926/) [V].
- **Nycz (2015).** Nycz, J. (2015), "Second dialect acquisition: A sociophonetic perspective," *Language and Linguistics Compass* 9(11), 469–482 — [dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/llc/Nycz15) [V, bibliographic]; DOI believed to be 10.1111/lnc3.12163 [PK].
- **Adult migrants change.** Research on adult migrants reports that they "do make changes in their linguistic production and perception upon constant exposure to a second dialect, though not all features prove susceptible to change" — [Sadeghi, *Canadian Journal of Linguistics* 57(3)](https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/canadian_journal_of_linguistics/v057/57.3.sadeghi.pdf) [V, secondary summary]. Gnevsheva & Szakay (Macquarie) find both L1 and L2 speakers show SDA, with L2 speakers showing larger increases — [Macquarie record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/l2-speakers-are-more-flexible-than-l1-speakers-in-second-dialect-/) [V].
- **Chambers (1992).** Chambers, J. K. (1992), "Dialect acquisition," *Language* 68(4), 673–705 [PK]. Lexical replacements are acquired early and fast. Complex phonological rules are acquired slowly. Age of arrival matters. A morphological form like "-stes" is a lexically and morphologically salient item of the kind acquired relatively early [inference from PK].
- **Social priming of dialect.** Hay, J., Nolan, A., & Drager, K. (2006), "From fush to feesh: Exemplar priming in speech perception," *The Linguistic Review* 23(3), 351–379 [PK]. Merely exposing listeners to a symbol of a region (stuffed kangaroo vs. kiwi toys) shifted their perception toward that region's vowels. Dialect representations are indexed to social categories and can be activated by cues tied to those categories.
- **Perspective-taking predicts mimicry.** Chartrand & Bargh (1999), Experiment 3: dispositionally empathic/perspective-taking individuals showed more chameleon effect [PK for experiment detail; paper at [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10402679/)]. Maddux et al. (2008) link strategic mimicry with perspective-taking in negotiation — [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2008/chameleons-bake-bigger-pies-and-take-bigger-pieces-strategic) [V].
- **Affiliation goals.** Lakin & Chartrand (2003): affiliation goals increase mimicry ([doi:10.1111/1467-9280.14481](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.14481)) [PK].
- **Imagined interactions (Honeycutt).** Imagined interactions are mental conversations with significant others, treated as social cognition and mental imagery grounded in symbolic interactionism. Their listed functions include **rehearsal**, self-understanding, relational maintenance, conflict management, catharsis and compensation. Rehearsal activates cognitive scripts for anticipated conversations — [summary page](https://ipfs.aleph.im/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Imagined_interaction.html); [Bryant Digital Commons (Honeycutt book)](https://digitalcommons.bryant.edu/comm_books/32) [V, secondary; Wikipedia mirror]. Primary source believed to be Honeycutt, J. M. (2003), *Imagined Interactions: Daydreaming about Communication* (Hampton Press) [PK].
- **Imagined contact.** Crisp, R. J., & Turner, R. N. (2009), "Can imagined interactions produce positive perceptions? Reducing prejudice through simulated social contact," *American Psychologist* 64(4), 231–240 — [doi:10.1037/a0014718](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014718) [PK]. Mentally simulating an interaction with an out-group member produces some effects of real contact. This is tangential, but it shows that mental simulation of interlocutors has measurable social consequences.
- **Simulation as the comprehension mechanism.** Pickering & Garrod (2013) — see §3.

### Inferences
- The "chegastes" episode fits a chain supported by separate literatures, though not tested as a whole:
  1. A year of exposure stored paraense exemplars (SDA, exemplar theory).
  2. Liking or affiliation toward paraense friends raised readiness to converge (CAT; Lakin & Chartrand).
  3. Imagining a conversation with the paraense interlocutor (imagined interaction, simulation) activated that person's dialect "voice" (Pickering & Garrod 2013; Hay et al. 2006).
  4. Output aligned to the simulated partner (interactive alignment), producing the colloquial regional form rather than the normative "chegaste."
- That the form was the **vernacular paraense** variant ("-stes"), not the school norm, suggests learning by naturalistic exposure rather than conscious imitation of a textbook "tu" paradigm.

### Gaps
- No experimental study was found showing that *imagining* a specific interlocutor shifts a speaker's *production* of dialect features. Hay et al. concern perception, and Honeycutt does not measure dialect. This link remains an inference.
- No SDA study was found for Brazilian internal migration or contact between Minas Gerais and Pará.

## 5. Brazilian Portuguese sociolinguistics: "tu" with second-person agreement in Belém/Pará, and the "-stes" preterite (fizestes, chegastes)

### Takeaway
Pará (especially Belém) is one of the Brazilian areas where **tu** is widely used, and often with second-person verb agreement. That sets it apart from the large "você-only" region that includes most of the Southeast. Studies describe Belém's agreement as either "commonly standard" or "variable," and one researcher ties the standard agreement to schooling. The colloquial preterite with added -s ("tu falastes", "tu fostes", "chegastes") is attested in Pará data. Normatively it is the *vós* plural form; descriptively it is an analogical second-person -s. No dedicated quantitative study of "-stes" in Belém was found.

### Cited Findings
- **National map.** Scherre et al. (2015) map tu/você across Brazil into **six subsystems** that combine tu frequency with agreement rates ("você/tu sem concordância," etc.), and report a large region where tu is not used at all — [Confluência article citing Scherre et al.](https://www.revistaconfluencia.org.br/rc/article/download/1447/1176/4152); [UFSC Working Papers](https://periodicos.ufsc.br/index.php/workingpapers/article/download/1984-8420.2017v18n2p171/35836/184237) [V, secondary]. - **Normative status of "-stes".** In normative grammar the 2sg preterite is *fizeste/chegaste*, and *fizestes/chegastes* is the 2pl (*vós*) form. ### Inferences
- "-stes" is best described as **analogical extension of the 2sg marker -s**. The -s marks the second person singular in every other tense (falas, falavas, falarás, falasses), and the preterite *-ste* is the only exception. Speakers who keep tu-agreement regularize it to *-stes*. This is a standard morphological analysis but was not sourced in this session (flag).
- For the case: a mineiro producing **"chegastes"** has acquired not just "tu" but the **specific vernacular paraense agreement variant**, rather than the school-normative *chegaste* or the Southeast's *você chegou* / Southern *tu chegou*. That is a fine-grained, socially indexical feature. It signals in-group familiarity much more strongly than generic "tu" would.
- Because "-stes" is stigmatized in prescriptive terms, a listener from Pará is likely to read it as *intimate/vernacular* rather than formal. That fits symmetric, friendly relations, consistent with Coan & Costa's finding that tu predominates in symmetric relations.

## 6. Social identity approach to leadership: why being "one of us" produces charisma attributions

### Takeaway
In the social identity / self-categorization approach, charisma is not a trait the leader possesses but a quality **followers bestow on leaders who are prototypical of, and stand up for, a shared "we."** Experimental, meta-analytic (k = 128) and multi-country (20+ countries) evidence supports this. Leaders who actively craft a shared identity ("identity entrepreneurs") are more influential.

### Cited Findings
- **Platow et al. (2006).** Platow, M. J., van Knippenberg, D., Haslam, S. A., van Knippenberg, B., & Spears, R. (2006), "A special gift we bestow on you for being representative of us: Considering leader charisma from a self-categorization perspective," *British Journal of Social Psychology* 45(2), 303–320 — [doi:10.1348/014466605X41986](https://doi.org/10.1348/014466605X41986); [EUR repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/11843); [ANU record](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/a-special-gift-we-bestow-on-you-for-being-representative-of-us-co/) [V].
  - Experiment 1: in-group-**prototypical** leaders were attributed more charisma and judged more persuasive than non-prototypical in-group leaders.
  - Experiment 2: leaders with out-group-stereotypical traits needed **group-oriented rhetoric** to be seen as charismatic.
  - Conclusion: charisma is "bestowed on group members by group members for being representative of, rather than distinct from, the group itself" [V, abstract].
- **The book.** Haslam, S. A., Reicher, S. D., & Platow, M. J. (2011), *The New Psychology of Leadership: Identity, Influence and Power* (Hove/New York: Psychology Press); 2nd edition 2020 (London/New York: Routledge) [PK]. Leaders succeed as in-group prototypes ("being one of us"), in-group champions ("doing it for us"), entrepreneurs of identity ("crafting a sense of us") and embedders of identity ("making us matter").
- **Identity Leadership Inventory (ILI).** Steffens, N. K., Haslam, S. A., Reicher, S. D., Platow, M. J., Fransen, K., Yang, J., Ryan, M. K., Jetten, J., Peters, K., & Boen, F. (2014), "Leadership as social identity management: Introducing the Identity Leadership Inventory (ILI) to assess and validate a four-dimensional model," *The Leadership Quarterly* 25(5), 1001–1024 — [doi:10.1016/j.leaqua.2014.05.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.leaqua.2014.05.002) [PK for details; the 2014 dating of the ILI is confirmed in [secondary source](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/163285/)]. Four dimensions: identity prototypicality, advancement, entrepreneurship and impresarioship.
- **20-country validation.** van Dick, R., Lemoine, J. E., Steffens, N. K., et al. (2018), "Identity leadership going global: Validation of the Identity Leadership Inventory across 20 countries," *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* 91(4), 697–728 — [doi:10.1111/joop.12223](https://doi.org/10.1111/joop.12223); [White Rose PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/163285/1/Identity%20leadership%20going%20global_Validation%20of%20the%20Identity%20Leadership%20Inventory%20across%2020%20countries.pdf) [V]. N = 5,290 across all six continents and more than 20 countries/regions. Consistent construct, discriminant and criterion validity; a short form is also valid. (Whether Brazil was among the countries was not verified.)
- **Meta-analysis of prototypicality.** Steffens, N. K., Munt, K. A., van Knippenberg, D., Platow, M. J., & Haslam, S. A. (2021), "Advancing the social identity theory of leadership: A meta-analytic review of leader group prototypicality," *Organizational Psychology Review* 11(1), 35–72 — [doi:10.1177/2041386620962569](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2041386620962569); [ANU](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/advancing-the-social-identity-theory-of-leadership-a-meta-analyti/) [V].
  - k = 128 studies, N = 32,834.
  - A **moderate-to-large** effect of prototypicality on evaluative and behavioural leadership outcomes.
  - The effect does not depend on prototypicality implying differentiation from out-groups.
  - Moderators: follower identification **strengthens** the effect; leader group-serving behaviour **attenuates** it (it can partly substitute for prototypicality).
- An earlier meta-analysis: Barreto, N. B., & Hogg, M. A. (2017), "Evaluation of and support for group prototypical leaders: A meta-analysis of twenty years of empirical research," *Social Influence* 12(1), 41–55 [PK].
- **"We"-language and electoral success.** Steffens, N. K., & Haslam, S. A. (2013), "Power through 'us': Leaders' use of we-referencing language predicts election victory," *PLoS ONE* 8(10), e77952 — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0077952](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0077952); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3806781) [V].
  - Data: campaign speeches from all 43 Australian federal elections since 1901.
  - Winners used more collective pronouns than losers in **80%** of elections, and **61% more** "we/us" overall (one per 79 words vs one per 136).
  - Each +1 SD of collective references was associated with ~124% higher odds of winning.
  - Correlational, single-country.
- **Theory of identity entrepreneurship.** Reicher, S., & Hopkins, N. (2001), *Self and Nation* (London: Sage); Reicher, S., Haslam, S. A., & Hopkins, N. (2005), "Social identity and the dynamics of leadership: Leaders and followers as collaborative agents in the transformation of social reality," *The Leadership Quarterly* 16(4), 547–568 — [doi:10.1016/j.leaqua.2005.06.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.leaqua.2005.06.007) [PK]. Leaders are "entrepreneurs of identity" who define who "we" are so that they and their proposals appear to embody the group. Also Hogg, M. A. (2001), "A social identity theory of leadership," *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 5(3), 184–200 — [doi:10.1207/S15327957PSPR0503_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327957PSPR0503_1) [PK].
- **Charismatic leadership tactics.** Antonakis, J., Fenley, M., & Liechti, S. (2011), "Can charisma be taught? Tests of two interventions," *Academy of Management Learning & Education* 10(3), 374–396 [PK]. The tactics include expressing the "sentiments of the collective." This links trainable verbal tactics to the same "we" mechanism (likely covered in more depth by other researchers).
- **Common Ingroup Identity Model.** Gaertner, S. L., & Dovidio, J. F. (2000), *Reducing Intergroup Bias: The Common Ingroup Identity Model* (Philadelphia: Psychology Press) [PK]. Recategorizing "us vs them" into an inclusive superordinate "we" reduces bias and increases positive orientation toward former out-group members.

### Inferences
- Saying "Deus" in a setting framed by the Lord's Prayer **raises perceived prototypicality**. The speaker appears to share the audience's defining frame, which by Platow et al. (2006) and Steffens et al. (2021) increases charisma attribution and persuasiveness. Platow's Experiment 2 is especially relevant: a speaker whose "traits" might mark them as out-group (a Candomblé practitioner, if known) can compensate with group-oriented rhetoric.
- "Deus" also works as a **superordinate category** (believers in God / people of faith) in the sense of the Common Ingroup Identity Model, rather than a pure substitution. In Candomblé the supreme deity is Olodumare/Olorum and Exu is an orixá-messenger [PK; Prandi 2001 calls Exu "mensageiro," see §10]. Speaking at the level of "Deus" can be truthful for the speaker *and* inclusive for the audience. That distinguishes it from claiming a Christian identity (see §10).
- The reported "intense attention" fits the identity account: messages from a perceived in-group member are processed as self-relevant. This is an inference; no study of audience attention was found that manipulates the speaker's religious-identity cues.

### Gaps
- Whether Brazil was included in van Dick et al. (2018) was not verified.
- No Brazilian Portuguese edition of *The New Psychology of Leadership* was found.
- No study was found that specifically tests religious-identity cues by a speaker in workplace talks on charisma attribution.

## 7. Rhetorical theory: Burke's identification, Perelman & Olbrechts-Tyteca's *auditório*, and Aristotle's ethos/pathos/logos and kairos

### Takeaway
Classical and modern rhetoric anticipated the psychology. Burke makes **identification** ("consubstantiality") the core of persuasion. Perelman & Olbrechts-Tyteca define argumentation relative to an **auditório** the speaker constructs and must adapt to. Aristotle grounds ethos in the speech itself and advises fitting arguments to the audience's character and values.

### Cited Findings
- **Burke's quote.** The passage is reproduced in a Bradley University study guide in Burke's discussion of identification as supplementing (or even replacing) persuasion — [Bradley guide](https://interactivemedia.bradley.edu/ell/burke.html) [V, exact-phrase search match]. The wording returned for the exact-phrase query: **"You persuade a man only insofar as you can talk his language by speech, gesture, tonality, order, image, attitude, idea, identifying your ways with his."** Source: Kenneth Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives* (1950; University of California Press ed. 1969).
  - **Page number not confirmed.** It is commonly cited as p. 55 of the 1969 UC Press edition [PK]. Check the book.
  - UC Press edition page: [UC Press](https://ucpress.edu/books/a-rhetoric-of-motives/paper); De Gruyter e-book: [doi:10.1525/9780520353237](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1525/9780520353237/html) [V].
- **Identification as Burke's key term.** "The key term for rhetoric in Kenneth Burke's usage is not persuasion, but identification" — [Taylor & Francis chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9781315080925/chapters/10.4324/9781315080925-15) [V, secondary]. Burke's related concepts are *consubstantiality* (sharing substance with the audience) and identification arising *because of* division [PK; exact quotes not verified].
- **Perelman & Olbrechts-Tyteca: definition of *auditório*.** In the Brazilian edition the *auditório* is "o conjunto daqueles que o orador quer influenciar com sua argumentação" (TA, p. 22) — [UBI Rhetorike issue](https://ojs.labcom-ifp.ubi.pt/rhetorike/issue/download/46/2); [Wayhs, BOCC](https://arquivo.bocc.ubi.pt/pag/wayhs-joao-convencimento-e-persuasao.pdf) [V, quoted in secondary sources; page varies by edition]. Original: *Traité de l'argumentation: la nouvelle rhétorique* (1958).
  - Brazilian edition: *Tratado da argumentação: a nova retórica*, trad. Maria Ermantina Galvão G. Pereira (São Paulo: Martins Fontes, 1996). A later edition (2005) has a preface by Michel Meyer [V, per secondary sources above].
  - Their distinction: argument to a **particular audience** is associated with *persuasão*, argument to the **universal audience** with *convencimento*. All audiences are **constructed by the speaker** — [same sources](https://arquivo.bocc.ubi.pt/pag/wayhs-joao-convencimento-e-persuasao.pdf); [UESC Eidea](https://periodicos.uesc.br/index.php/eidea/article/download/412/420) [V, secondary].
  - The treatise also has sections on "the audience as a construction of the speaker" and on "adaptation of the speaker to the audience" (§§4–5) [PK; section numbers not verified].
- **Aristotle, *Rhetoric*.** No URL verified in session; standard Bekker references from prior knowledge [PK]:
  - Three *pisteis*: ēthos (the speaker's character as conveyed **by the speech itself**, 1356a), pathos (putting the audience in a frame of mind) and logos (1356a1–20).
  - In Book I.9 (1367b), on epideictic, Aristotle cites Socrates (cf. Plato, *Menexenus* 235d) that it is not difficult to praise Athenians among Athenians, and advises speaking to what the particular audience esteems.
  - Book II.12–17 profiles audience "characters" (young, old, wealthy, powerful) so that speakers can adapt.
  - Portuguese edition: Aristóteles, *Retórica*, trad. Manuel Alexandre Júnior, Paulo Farmhouse Alberto & Abel do Nascimento Pena (Lisboa: Imprensa Nacional-Casa da Moeda; also São Paulo: WMF Martins Fontes, 2012) [PK; not verified].
- **Kairos.** Kairos (the opportune moment and fitness to the occasion) is a sophistic/classical concept that Aristotle treats only implicitly. A standard modern source is Kinneavy, J. L. (1986), "Kairos: A neglected concept in classical rhetoric," in J. D. Moss (ed.), *Rhetoric and Praxis* (Washington: Catholic University of America Press) [PK; not verified].

### Inferences
- Burke's list ("speech, gesture, tonality, order, image, attitude, idea") is a pre-empirical list of accommodation dimensions. "Chegastes" is identification at the level of *speech*, and "Deus" at the level of *idea/image*.
- Perelman's point that the speaker constructs the audience matters for the "Deus" case. The Lord's Prayer told the speaker which particular *auditório* was present, and adapting to it is, in Perelman's terms, a condition of argument rather than a trick. Perelman's universal-audience standard also gives an ethical check: could the speaker defend the choice before *all* reasonable listeners, including Candomblé practitioners? This connects to Bok's publicity test (§10).
- Kairos fits the timing: the moment right after a collective prayer is the opportune point for a shared-faith reference.

- The Perelman page number (p. 22) is from a secondary citation, and the edition varies.

## 8. Erving Goffman: self-presentation, face, footing and frames as tools for adapting to situations

### Takeaway
Goffman supplies the sociological vocabulary for adapting to an audience.
- Performances are tailored to audiences (front/back stage).
- Actors work to protect their own and others' **face**.
- Speakers shift **footing** (alignment toward the interaction).
- **Frames** define "what is going on here."
- His notions of **audience segregation**, sincere vs cynical performers, and passing/covering (from *Stigma*) bear directly on the ethics of the "Deus" choice.

### Cited Findings
- ***The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*** (1959, New York: Doubleday Anchor; first ed. Edinburgh 1956) [PK].
  - Concepts: performance, front/back region, "teams," and "audience segregation": performers keep different audiences apart so that those who see one performance do not see another.
  - The **sincere** performer (who believes in their own act) vs the **cynical** one.
  - Brazilian edition: *A representação do eu na vida cotidiana*, trad. Maria Célia Santos Raposo (Petrópolis: Vozes; first ed. 1975) [PK; edition not verified in session].
- ***Interaction Ritual: Essays on Face-to-Face Behavior*** (1967) collects six essays previously published in journals, including "On face-work" (1955) and "The nature of deference and demeanor" (1956). Brazilian edition: *Ritual de interação: ensaios sobre o comportamento face a face* (Petrópolis: Vozes, 2011) — [Veredas (UFJF) article](https://periodicos.ufjf.br/index.php/veredas/article/view/35124/23257) [V].
- **"Footing"** (1979, *Semiotica* 25:1–29; reprinted in ***Forms of Talk***, 1981, Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press). Footing is the alignment speakers take up to themselves and others, signalled by shifts in code, register, tone or pronouns.
  - Portuguese: "Footing," in B. T. Ribeiro & P. M. Garcez (orgs.), *Sociolinguística interacional* (São Paulo: Loyola, 2ª ed. 2002, pp. 107–148; earlier ed. Porto Alegre: AGE, 1998). Ribeiro & Garcez render *footing* as "alinhamento, postura, posição" — [UnB *Cadernos de Linguagem e Sociedade* article](https://periodicos.unb.br/index.php/les/article/view/6490); [Gelne/UFRN](https://periodicos.ufrn.br/gelne/article/download/33575/18309/122381) [V].
- ***Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience*** (1974, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press) — referenced in [Tempo Social (USP), "A sociolingüística de Goffman e a comunicação mediada"](https://revistas.usp.br/ts/article/view/12555) [V]. Brazilian edition believed to be *Os quadros da experiência social: uma perspectiva de análise* (Petrópolis: Vozes, 2012) [PK; NOT verified].
- ***Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity*** (1963, Englewood Cliffs: Prentice-Hall) [PK]. It distinguishes **passing** (concealing a discreditable attribute) from **covering** (making a known attribute less obtrusive). Brazilian edition: *Estigma: notas sobre a manipulação da identidade deteriorada* (Rio de Janeiro: Zahar/LTC) [PK]. Yoshino, K. (2006), *Covering: The Hidden Assault on Our Civil Rights* (New York: Random House), extends "covering" to workplaces [PK].

### Inferences
- The Lord's Prayer opening **framed** the event (Goffman 1974) as a religiously-inflected company ritual. Saying "Deus" keeps the speaker *within* that frame, whereas "Exu" would break it. Breaking the frame could have generated face threat for the audience and the speaker, especially given the historical demonization of Exu (§10).
- "Chegastes" is a **footing** shift: the code switch to paraense morphology aligns the speaker as an intimate/insider toward the addressee.
- Goffman's typology gives the ethics section its core question. Is "Deus" **covering** (downplaying a religion that may be known), **passing** (implying the speaker is Christian), or simply a **sincere** performance at a level of generality the speaker believes in? Audience segregation also explains the main risk: the problem arises if audiences later "see the other performance" (§10).

## 9. Rapport: Tickle-Degnen & Rosenthal (1990)

### Takeaway
Rapport has three components: **mutual attentiveness, positivity and coordination**. Their weighting shifts over a relationship: positivity matters most early, coordination grows in importance later, and attentiveness stays constant. The speaker's report of intense audience attention describes the *attentiveness* component; dialect alignment and shared vocabulary are forms of *coordination*.

### Cited Findings
- Tickle-Degnen, L., & Rosenthal, R. (1990), "The nature of rapport and its nonverbal correlates," *Psychological Inquiry* 1(4), 285–293 — [doi:10.1207/s15327965pli0104_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327965pli0104_1) [PK]. Components as above.
  - Nonverbal correlates: attentiveness is shown through mutual gaze, forward lean and orientation; positivity through smiling and nodding; coordination through synchrony and posture similarity.
  - Proposed developmental model: positivity is high early and declines, coordination increases with familiarity, and attentiveness is constant.
- Mimicry and LSM research (§3) operationalizes the *coordination* component. Its effects on liking or rapport are real but smaller and more conditional than early work suggested (FORRT replication atlas; Bierstetel et al. 2020 IDA) — [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.76.6.893/); [JSPR 2020](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/bierstetel_et_al_jspr_2020.pdf) [V].

### Inferences
- In a one-off lecture to strangers, the 1990 model predicts that **positivity** (warmth, shared-value signals) and **attentiveness** dominate. Signalling shared faith early boosts positivity, and the reported attentiveness is consistent with rapport forming. With a long-term paraense friend, **coordination** (shared dialect forms) becomes the more prominent sign of rapport, as the model predicts for established relationships.

### Gaps
- I could not access the 1990 paper in this session. The component details are from prior knowledge.
- No later large-scale test of the three-component developmental model was located.

## 10. Ethics and limits: when does adaptation (e.g., replacing a religious term) become concealment or manipulation?

### Takeaway
Research does not supply a bright line, but it identifies risk factors:
- **implied false identity claims** (false signalling), which are judged more harshly than plain lies;
- adaptation driven by **ingratiation toward power**;
- **discovery** by an audience that was meant to be segregated;
- the **internal costs of concealment** to the speaker, including "saying-is-believing" self-change.

Bok's publicity test and Perelman's universal audience give practical checks. Workplace research shows religious identity is generally a net positive at work when an inclusive climate allows its expression. In Brazil, the history of Exu's demonization makes the speaker's choice understandable but also politically loaded.

### Cited Findings
- **Hypocrisy as false signalling.** Jordan, J. J., Sommers, R., Bloom, P., & Rand, D. G. (2017), "Why do we hate hypocrites? Evidence for a theory of false signaling," *Psychological Science* 28(3), 356–368 — [doi:10.1177/0956797616685771](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797616685771/); [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/we-dislike-hypocrites-because-they-deceive-us.html) [V].
  - Hypocrites are disliked because their condemnation falsely signals their own virtue, and they were judged **more harshly than people who directly lie** about their behaviour.
  - "Honest hypocrites" who admit the inconsistency are not penalized as heavily.
- **Code-switching at work is rewarded but costly.** McCluney, C. L., Durkee, M. I., Smith, R., Robotham, K. J., & Lee, S. S.-L. (2021), "To be, or not to be…Black: The effects of racial codeswitching on perceived professionalism in the workplace," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 97, 104199 — [Michigan Ross summary](https://positiveorgs.bus.umich.edu/articles/to-be-or-not-to-beblack-the-effects-of-racial-codeswitching-on-perceived-professionalism-in-the-workplace/); [Cornell news](https://news.cornell.edu/node/322261) [V].
  - Both Black and White raters judged the code-switching coworker as more professional.
  - Participants endorsed adjusting speech, opposed changing one's name, and diverged on natural hairstyles.
  - The authors warn that the practice may reinforce dominant-group standards and impose psychological costs.
- **Religious identity at work.** Héliot, Y., Gleibs, I. H., Coyle, A., Rousseau, D. M., & Rojon, C. (2020), "Religious identity in the workplace: A systematic review, research agenda, and practical implications," *Human Resource Management* 59(2), 153–173 — [doi:10.1002/hrm.21983](https://doi.org/10.1002/hrm.21983); [LSE Research Online](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/100950); [Kingston PDF](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/43464/1/Coyle-A-43464-VoR.pdf) [V].
  - A review of 53 publications.
  - Religious identity tends to be a **net benefit** (citizenship behaviour, ethical conduct).
  - Outcomes depend on personal preferences, fit between religious identity and job concerns, and organizational policies and expectations.
  - The authors recommend supporting religious expression within a broad climate of inclusion.
- **The Brazilian context of Exu.** Prandi, R. (2001), "Exu, de mensageiro a diabo: sincretismo católico e demonização do orixá Exu," *Revista USP* 50, 46–63 (author's PDF gives 46–65) — [doi:10.11606/issn.2316-9036.v0i50p46-63](https://doi.org/10.11606/issn.2316-9036.v0i50p46-63); [Revista USP](https://revistas.usp.br/revusp/article/view/35275); [author PDF](https://reginaldoprandi.fflch.usp.br/sites/reginaldoprandi.fflch.usp.br/files/inline-files/Exu_de_mensageiro_a_diabo.pdf) [V].
  - Early European observers in Africa gave Exu a dual identity, Priapus-like fertility god and the Judeo-Christian devil.
  - Catholic syncretism in Brazil consolidated the **demonization** of Exu, originally the orixá-messenger.
  - Later work builds on this (e.g., *Revista de Antropologia* 2012; *Mneme* 2020) — [Mneme](https://periodicos.ufrn.br/mneme/article/download/22113/17721/118842) [V].
- **Bok's publicity test.** Bok, S. (1978), *Lying: Moral Choice in Public and Private Life* (New York: Pantheon) [PK]. Bok proposes a **test of publicity** (drawing on Kant and on Rawls's publicity condition): a deception is justifiable only if it could be defended openly before reasonable people, including those deceived. *No exact quotation was verified in this session; paraphrase only.*
- **Ingratiation and the "slime effect."** Vonk, R. (1998), "The slime effect: Suspicion and dislike of likeable behavior toward superiors," *JPSP* 74(4), 849–864 — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.74.4.849](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.4.849) [PK]. Observers strongly dislike people who are agreeable toward superiors but not toward subordinates. Adaptation perceived as power-directed ingratiation is penalized.
- **Costs of concealment.** Newheiser, A.-K., & Barreto, M. (2014), "Hidden costs of hiding stigma: Ironic interpersonal consequences of concealing a stigmatized identity in social interactions," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 52, 58–70 [PK]. Concealing a stigmatized identity reduced the concealer's sense of belonging and could make interactions *less* positive. Concealment has relational costs, not only benefits.
- **"Saying is believing."** Higgins, E. T., & Rholes, W. S. (1978), "'Saying is believing': Effects of message modification on memory and liking for the person described," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 14(4), 363–378 [PK]. Echterhoff, G., Higgins, E. T., & Levine, J. M. (2009), "Shared reality: Experiencing commonality with others' inner states about the world," *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 4(5), 496–521 [PK]. Speakers who tune messages to an audience later remember and believe the tuned version, especially when they want a shared reality with that audience. Repeated audience-tuning can reshape the speaker's own representations.
- **Covering at work.** Yoshino (2006) argues that pressure to "cover" (downplay) disfavoured identities is a pervasive and under-recognized workplace demand [PK].

### Inferences
- A workable decision rule synthesized from these sources (offered as synthesis, not a finding):
  - **Low risk (inclusive generalization):** the term chosen is one the speaker sincerely holds at that level of generality ("Deus" as the supreme deity, Olodumare/Olorum, which Candomblé recognizes), and the speaker does not *claim* a Christian identity.
  - **Higher risk (false signalling, passing):** the speaker *implies* membership ("as Christians, we…"). Jordan et al. suggest this would be punished more than an open difference if discovered, while "honest" disclosure reduces the penalty.
  - **Ingratiation risk:** adaptation flows only "upward," toward those with power over the speaker (Vonk 1998).
  - **Publicity test (Bok):** would the speaker be comfortable if the audience learned that he practises Candomblé and chose "Deus" deliberately? Would Candomblé peers see it as respectful translation or as erasure? If both answers are yes/acceptable, the adaptation is defensible.
- **Structural context:** Prandi documents Exu's demonization, and McCluney et al. and Yoshino show that minority speakers bear the cost of adapting to majority norms. The speaker's choice may be prudent self-protection *and* an instance of the covering pressure that inclusion research criticizes. The report should present both readings.
- **Long-run risk:** if adaptation becomes habitual, "saying-is-believing" and concealment-cost research suggest possible erosion of the speaker's own identity salience and well-being.

### Gaps
- **No study was found that directly tests audience reactions on discovering that a speaker substituted a religious term to fit in.** The closest evidence is the hypocrisy/false-signalling and ingratiation research.
- Bok's exact wording and page number were not verified.
- No Brazilian empirical study on religious-identity management by Afro-Brazilian religion practitioners in corporate settings was searched for or found in this session (a possible follow-up).
- The details of Newheiser & Barreto (2014) and Vonk (1998) are from prior knowledge.

## 11. How the mechanisms explain the two reported episodes (synthesis question)

### Takeaway
Both episodes are cases of **convergence toward an interlocutor's or audience's identity markers**:
- "chegastes" is morpho-dialectal alignment produced automatically after long exposure, triggered by imagining the addressee;
- "Deus" is a deliberate appeal to the audience's communal common ground that raises perceived prototypicality.

The research predicts warmth and attention when convergence is calibrated and its motive is read as affiliative. It predicts penalties when convergence is exaggerated, ingratiating, or later revealed as a false identity claim.

### Cited Findings
- Charisma as bestowed for representativeness, with group-oriented rhetoric compensating for out-group traits: Platow et al. (2006) — [doi:10.1348/014466605X41986](https://doi.org/10.1348/014466605X41986) [V].
- Prototypicality has moderate-to-large effects on leadership outcomes (k = 128): Steffens et al. (2021) — [doi:10.1177/2041386620962569](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2041386620962569) [V].
- "We"-language is associated with electoral victory: Steffens & Haslam (2013) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3806781) [V].
- Convergence is evaluated through inferred motive: Gasiorek & Giles (2012) — [doi:10.1111/j.1468-2958.2012.01426.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2012.01426.x) [V, bibliographic].
- Paraense "tu" with "-stes" is attested in Pará speech data: Coan & Costa (2019) — [Moara](https://periodicos.ufpa.br/index.php/moara/article/download/8163/5995) [V]; Araguaia study — [Dialnet](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/10883774.pdf) [V].
- Tu is favoured in Belém: Costa (2013) — [UFPA](https://repositorio.ufpa.br/items/0c82660e-b036-4968-abaf-6e514deb0058) [V].
- Mimicry→liking is replicated only partially: [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.76.6.893/) [V].

### Inferences
- **Evidence strength, by link:**
  - *Strong* (meta-analytic, multi-country): being seen as "one of us" increases leader influence and charisma attributions.
  - *Strong descriptive*: people align linguistically in dialogue.
  - *Moderate*: listeners reward calibrated convergence and punish over-accommodation and perceived bad motives (older experimental CAT work plus a modern inferred-motive study).
  - *Weak/indirect*: imagining an interlocutor triggers production of their dialect (an inference combining simulation theory, social priming and imagined-interaction theory, not directly tested).
  - *No direct evidence*: reactions when an audience discovers a substituted religious term.
- **The "intense attention" report** is anecdotal self-report, open to confirmation bias. The literature makes it plausible through three routes, none tested in this setting:
  1. identity-relevance and prototypicality;
  2. rapport's attentiveness and positivity components;
  3. a frame-consistent speaker reducing audience face threat.
- **Practical boundary conditions:**
  - prefer partial, sincere and low-dose convergence;
  - converge "laterally" (peers) as well as "upward";
  - use superordinate shared categories rather than adopting another group's identity claims;
  - apply the publicity test.

### Gaps
- No study was found that combines dialect accommodation and identity leadership in Brazilian Portuguese settings.
- No direct test was found of charisma ratings for speakers who use *regional* (vs standard) Brazilian forms with audiences from that region.
